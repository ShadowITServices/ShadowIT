# Hacks

Most security writing about a new technique is either a vendor blog with a product
at the end of it, or a conference talk aimed at the person doing the attacking.
Neither is much use at nine on a Monday morning when you're the one who has to
decide whether it matters to your environment.

This section is the other thing: a plain-language breakdown of a technique, written
for the person holding the pager. Each page answers the same four questions.

- **What it actually does** — the mechanism, in concepts rather than commands.
- **Why it matters here** — the business consequence, not the CVSS score.
- **Why your existing controls miss it** — and where that claim comes from.
- **What to do about it** — detection you can build, and a containment step that
  works.

## The editorial line

<Admonition type="warning" title="No operational detail">

These pages describe mechanisms, not procedures. There are no commands, no code,
no parameters, and no step-by-step instructions for reproducing anything. Where a
technique has a public proof-of-concept, the original research is credited and
linked so you can read it in its own context — but this site isn't going to
assemble a working attack for you.

Anything you do test, you test on systems you own or have explicit written
authorisation to assess. In Australia that's not a disclaimer, it's Part 10.7 of
the *Criminal Code Act 1995*.

</Admonition>

A second rule: **claims get attributed.** A lot of technique write-ups quietly
launder a researcher's own lab numbers into established fact. Where a figure comes
from the original research rather than from independent verification, these pages
say so, every time. You can then decide how much weight it carries in your
environment, which is the decision you were trying to make anyway.

## Breakdowns

- [GhostLock — denial of availability without encryption](/hacks/ghost-lock) —
  a standard domain user with read access to a file share can make files
  unavailable to everyone else without writing a single byte. No malware, no
  encryption, no CVE, and almost nothing in your SIEM. The interesting part isn't
  the technique; it's how completely the ransomware-shaped assumptions baked into
  modern tooling miss it.

More will land here as they're worth writing up. The bar is that a technique has
to change what a defender should actually do — a clever trick that every control
already catches isn't worth your reading time.
